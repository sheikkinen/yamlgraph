The Salt Road

Naima is a salt trader's daughter in 14th-century Timbuktu, the youngest
person to hold a seat on the merchants' council. Her father Issa built the
family's position over forty years of caravan routes through the Sahara; she
inherited it three months ago when he collapsed at the counting table, his
heart giving out between one ledger entry and the next.

The problem arrives with the rains. Moussa Keita, the governor's nephew and
newly appointed tax collector, announces a royal salt monopoly: all private
salt concessions are forfeit to the crown. The caravans Naima dispatches will
carry salt for the governor's treasury, not her own. The council protests;
Moussa smiles and presents the royal seal. It is law now.

Naima knows the monopoly will destroy the small traders — her people — but she
has no leverage. The council votes to comply. Only old Diallo, her father's
rival and the most cautious man she knows, refuses to sign. He tells Naima
that a sealed letter from the old king — predating the monopoly decree —
grants the merchants' council an irrevocable charter. If the letter exists, the
monopoly is illegal. But the letter is in Djenné, three weeks upriver, locked
in the archive of the Great Mosque.

She decides to go. Amadou, her father's former caravan guide — a quiet Tuareg
who speaks four languages and trusts no cities — agrees to take her. They leave
at night, telling no one on the council. Moussa's agents are already watching
the river docks.

The journey upriver is brutal. Amadou's knowledge of the seasonal channels
saves them twice: once from a sandbar that would have grounded them for days,
once from a hippo territory that has killed three boats this season. They argue
constantly — Naima wants to push through the night; Amadou insists on resting
when the river narrows. By the time they reach Djenné, they have stopped
speaking to each other about anything except navigation.

At the Great Mosque, the archivist — an elderly scholar named Fatimata — knows
the letter. She has catalogued every document in the archive over thirty years.
But she will not simply hand it over. She tests Naima: recite the founding
charter of the merchants' council from memory. Naima's father made her memorize
it as a child. She recites it, stumbling on the seventh clause, then
correcting herself. Fatimata nods and leads her to the sealed vault.

The letter is there. It is clear, unambiguous, and bears the old king's seal.
Naima holds it and feels the weight of her father's voice in the words — he
fought for this charter before she was born.

The return is the dangerous part. Moussa has learned they left. His men are on
the river road. Amadou takes them overland through the dry country — no water
for two days, sleeping in dry wadis, moving only at night. On the second night,
they see torches behind them. Moussa's riders. Amadou tells Naima to go ahead
with the letter while he leads the riders away with a decoy fire.

Naima arrives in Timbuktu alone, dehydrated, the letter wrapped in oilcloth
against her ribs. She walks into the council hall during Moussa's collection
ceremony and presents the charter letter to the assembled merchants and the
governor's representative. The letter is read aloud. The monopoly is illegal.
Moussa protests, but the governor's own representative — who values the law
over his nephew — orders the decree suspended.

Diallo, who told her about the letter, rises and publicly acknowledges that
Issa's daughter has done what the council could not. The traders keep their
routes. Naima takes her father's seat at the council table — not as his
daughter, but as the one who earned it.

Amadou returns three days later, limping, his horse gone. He says nothing about
what happened. They share a meal in silence, and then he leaves for the desert
again.
